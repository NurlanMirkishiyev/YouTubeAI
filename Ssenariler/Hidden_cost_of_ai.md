## Scene 1 — The illusion of free intelligence
**Time:** 00:00–00:55

### Voiceover
Imagine hiring a brilliant assistant who can write emails, explain complicated ideas, analyze spreadsheets, create images, and help build software. Now imagine that assistant is available at any hour, can serve millions of people at once, and sometimes works for free.

It sounds like an incredible deal. And from the user's point of view, it often is.

But there is a catch: the assistant does not live inside your phone or laptop. Every time you send a request to a cloud-based AI service, powerful computers somewhere in the world may need to process it. Those computers consume electricity, require expensive hardware, generate heat, and need engineers to keep everything running.

So here is the business question: if intelligence feels so easy to access, why is providing it becoming such an expensive industry?

The answer reveals something important about the future of technology. AI is not just software. It is software sitting on top of a huge, physical infrastructure business.

## Scene 2 — AI has two very different bills
**Time:** 00:55–01:50

### Voiceover
To understand the cost, we need to separate two jobs that AI companies do.

The first is **training**. This is the process of building a model by exposing it to enormous amounts of data and adjusting its internal parameters so it can recognize patterns and generate useful responses. Training a leading model can require large clusters of specialized chips working together for long periods. It also involves research, experiments, data preparation, and repeated attempts that may not succeed.

The second job is **inference**. That is what happens after the model has been trained, when it answers your question, summarizes a document, writes code, or generates an image.

Training is like building and educating an expert. Inference is like paying that expert to answer questions every day.

A company may spend a huge amount to create a model, but it cannot stop paying once the model is launched. Every new request uses computing resources. And when millions of people use the product, those small costs can add up to a very large operating bill.

### Visual direction
- Split screen: “TRAINING” with a large chip cluster; “INFERENCE” with user requests flowing through servers.
- On-screen text: **“Training builds the model. Inference runs the service.”**

## Scene 3 — The chips behind the answer
**Time:** 01:50–02:45

### Voiceover
AI workloads need a lot of parallel computation. Traditional computer processors are excellent at many general tasks, but modern AI models often benefit from specialized accelerators, including GPUs and other AI chips.

The problem is not simply buying one chip. Large AI services need many of them, connected through high-speed networking, installed in racks, and supported by memory, storage, backup systems, and power equipment. The fastest hardware is in high demand, and building a large cluster takes planning, supply-chain coordination, and substantial capital.

There is also a utilization problem. A chip that sits idle is expensive capacity producing no useful output. But running every chip at maximum load is not always practical either: requests arrive unevenly, models have different memory needs, and users expect fast responses.

That means AI companies are trying to solve two problems at once: get powerful hardware, and keep it busy enough to justify the investment.

In this business, owning the machinery is only the beginning. The real challenge is turning that machinery into useful answers at a competitive cost.

### Visual direction
- Reveal server racks and glowing network links; an “idle / busy” utilization gauge moves.
- On-screen text: **“Expensive hardware must stay productive.”**

## Scene 4 — Electricity, cooling, and the physical world
**Time:** 02:45–03:45

### Voiceover
Here is the cost that is easy to forget: AI runs in the physical world.

Servers use electricity, and high-performance chips produce heat. Data centers need cooling systems, power distribution, networking, security, maintenance, and reliable connections to the electricity grid. These are not optional extras. Without them, the computers cannot safely operate at scale.

The International Energy Agency estimated that data centers used around 415 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2024, about 1.5 percent of global electricity consumption. Its 2025 analysis projected that total data-center electricity use could roughly double by 2030, although the actual path depends on efficiency, demand, and how quickly infrastructure is built.

Those figures cover data centers broadly, not AI alone. But they show the scale of the system AI is helping to expand.

And electricity is only part of the bill. A company may need new power connections, backup generation, cooling equipment, and a site capable of handling very dense computing hardware. In some locations, getting enough power can be as difficult as getting the chips.

AI may appear to be an invisible service. Its infrastructure is anything but invisible.

- On-screen statistic: **“Data centers: ~415 TWh in 2024”** with label “IEA estimate; all data centers.”
- Owl reacts to a hot server rack as cooling activates.

## Scene 5 — Why a longer answer can cost more
**Time:** 03:45–04:40

### Voiceover
AI services are often priced using something called **tokens**. A token is a small unit of text processed by a language model. It may be a word, part of a word, punctuation, or another text fragment. The exact amount varies by language and tokenization method.

When you send a prompt, the model processes input tokens. When it responds, it generates output tokens. Longer documents, larger context windows, and extended conversations can require more computation and memory.

But token count is not the whole story. A simple model answering a short question may be relatively inexpensive. A more advanced model working through a complex reasoning task, examining a large file, or using tools repeatedly can require far more resources.

Think of it like transportation. A short trip in a small car and a cross-country trip in a heavy truck are not priced the same just because both are called “a journey.”

For AI businesses, the important question is not just how many users they have. It is what those users ask the system to do, how much computing each task consumes, and how much revenue the company earns from that work.

### Visual direction
- Animate a short prompt producing a small stream of tokens and a long report producing a larger stream.
- Owl compares a bicycle ride with a cross-country truck journey.
- On-screen text: **“More tokens + more complex work = potentially higher cost.”**

## Scene 6 — AI agents change the cost equation
**Time:** 04:40–05:40

### Voiceover
The next generation of AI products is not limited to answering one question. AI agents may plan a task, search for information, call tools, inspect results, correct mistakes, and repeat steps until a job is complete.

That can be useful. An agent might help research a market, compare suppliers, draft a report, or work through a software task. But a multi-step workflow can trigger many model calls instead of just one.

Imagine asking an AI to find five competitors. That may take one request. Now ask it to find competitors, inspect their websites, compare their prices, check reviews, build a spreadsheet, identify patterns, and write a report. The system may need to perform a sequence of searches, tool calls, reasoning steps, and follow-up checks.

The result can be much more valuable, but it can also be much more expensive to deliver.

This creates a new business challenge: a company may charge a flat monthly subscription while some customers use the product lightly and others run complex tasks all day. If heavy usage costs more than the subscription brings in, growth can actually make the business less profitable.

The smarter AI products will need to manage both usefulness and cost.

- Counter rises from “1 request” to “many model calls.”
- Show a light user and a power user on the same subscription.
- On-screen text: **“More capable agents can mean more compute per task.”**

## Scene 7 — AI can get cheaper and still cost more
**Time:** 05:40–06:40

### Voiceover
Here is the paradox: the cost of using AI has fallen dramatically for many tasks, yet the industry's total spending on computing and infrastructure has continued to rise.

Stanford's 2025 AI Index reported that the cost of querying a model with performance comparable to GPT-3.5 on a benchmark fell from about twenty dollars per million tokens in November 2022 to around seven cents per million tokens by October 2024, using a much cheaper model in the comparison. The report also described major improvements in hardware price-performance and energy efficiency.

That does not mean every AI task costs seven cents per million tokens, or that all models have the same price. It illustrates how quickly the cost of a given level of performance can fall.

So why can spending still rise? Because lower prices can encourage more use. When a service becomes cheaper, businesses add it to more workflows, developers build new products, and customers ask AI to do more ambitious tasks. Image generation, video generation, long-context analysis, and agentic workflows can create demand that did not exist before.

This is a familiar economic pattern: efficiency makes each unit cheaper, but the number of units grows even faster.

The question is whether productivity and revenue grow quickly enough to cover the expanding infrastructure bill.

- Price tag falls while usage graph rises.
- Show use cases appearing: writing, coding, images, research, and agents.
- On-screen note: **“Illustrative benchmark comparison — not a universal AI price.”**
- On-screen text: **“Cheaper per task does not always mean cheaper overall.”**

## Scene 8 — The hidden cost of reliability and speed
**Time:** 06:40–07:35

### Voiceover
Users rarely think about latency, uptime, or reliability until something goes wrong. But businesses pay for those things.

If a customer asks an AI assistant a question, they expect a response quickly. If a company embeds AI into customer support, fraud detection, or a business workflow, slow or unavailable service can damage the customer experience and disrupt operations.

Meeting those expectations may require spare capacity, multiple regions, monitoring systems, safety checks, and engineering teams ready to handle failures. Some requests also need extra verification or moderation. Those safeguards can add time and computing cost, but removing them can create different risks.

There is a trade-off between cost, speed, quality, and reliability. A provider that tries to minimize cost at all times may deliver slow or inconsistent results. A provider that always uses the largest and most expensive model may waste money on simple tasks.

This is why AI infrastructure is becoming an optimization problem. Companies need to decide which model should handle which task, how much capacity to keep available, and when a more expensive answer is actually worth paying for.

## Scene 9 — Why free AI is not really free
**Time:** 07:35–08:30

### Voiceover
If every request has a cost, why do some AI tools offer free plans?

Because free access can be a business strategy. It helps a company attract users, demonstrate what the product can do, and encourage people to build habits around it. Some users may later upgrade to a paid tier. Businesses may pay for team features, higher usage limits, stronger models, security controls, or integrations.

Other products may bundle AI into a wider subscription or use it to make an existing service more valuable. In those cases, the AI feature does not have to generate all of its revenue directly; it can help retain customers or sell another product.

But the economics depend on the mix of users. A free tier can work when usage is limited, infrastructure is efficient, or paid customers support the service. It becomes harder when heavy users consume large amounts of computing capacity without generating enough revenue.

That is why free plans often have usage caps, slower access during busy periods, or limits on advanced features. These limits are not necessarily signs that the product is failing. They can be ways to keep the service economically sustainable.

The real question is not “Is AI free?” It is “Who pays, for what, and under what limits?”

- On-screen text: **“Free access is often customer acquisition, not zero cost.”**

## Scene 10 — The investment risk behind the AI boom
**Time:** 08:30–09:30

### Voiceover
The infrastructure required for AI is so expensive that companies are making enormous long-term bets. They invest in chips, data centers, power capacity, and networking today, hoping that future demand will generate enough revenue to justify the spending.

That creates a timing problem. Infrastructure costs arrive before all the revenue does. A data center may take years to plan and build, while demand, model efficiency, and customer preferences can change much faster.

If demand grows as expected, the investment may look brilliant. If customers use less computing than forecast, prices fall faster than costs, or competitors offer similar capabilities more cheaply, some investments may earn disappointing returns.

This does not prove that AI is a bubble. It means the business case must be tested, not assumed.

A company can have impressive user growth and still struggle to make money if each user is expensive to serve. It can also spend heavily today to build a capability that becomes highly profitable later. Both outcomes are possible.

The key is to separate the value of AI as a technology from the financial performance of each company selling it.

## Scene 11 — How AI companies try to control the bill
**Time:** 09:30–10:30

### Voiceover
AI companies are not simply accepting these costs. They have several ways to reduce the cost of each useful result.

First, they can route tasks to different models. A small, fast model may handle classification or a simple rewrite, while a more powerful model is reserved for complex reasoning.

Second, they can improve software efficiency. Better batching, caching repeated results, smarter memory management, and optimized serving systems can help hardware produce more useful output.

Third, they can use smaller specialized models for narrow jobs. A model built for one task may be cheaper and faster than a general-purpose model that knows how to do many things.

Fourth, they can reduce unnecessary work. Better prompts, shorter context where appropriate, and fewer repeated agent steps can cut waste without hurting the result.

Finally, they can improve hardware utilization and choose where workloads run. Every improvement matters when a service handles enormous volumes of requests.

For customers, the lesson is simple: the best AI system is not always the biggest model. It is the system that achieves the required quality at a cost the business can afford.

- Owl routes tasks to the most suitable model.
- On-screen text: **“Use the right model for the right job.”**

## Scene 12 — What this means for ordinary businesses
**Time:** 10:30–11:30

### Voiceover
For a small business, AI can still be a powerful opportunity. The important step is to calculate the economics before adding AI everywhere.

Start with the job. Is AI saving employee time, increasing sales, reducing errors, or improving customer service? Then measure the cost of the complete workflow, not just the price of one model call. Include tool usage, software subscriptions, human review, failed attempts, and the time needed to maintain the system.

Next, compare the cost with the value created. If an AI workflow costs fifty dollars a month but saves ten hours of valuable work, it may be worthwhile. If it costs hundreds of dollars and produces results that require extensive correction, it may not be.

These numbers are only an illustration; actual costs and the value of time vary by business. The principle is what matters: measure outcomes rather than being impressed by a demo.

Businesses should also consider privacy, security, and reliability. Sending sensitive information to an AI service may require additional controls or a different deployment approach.

The winning strategy is not to use the most AI. It is to use AI where the measurable benefit exceeds the full cost and risk.

- Owl reviews a dashboard: “Monthly cost,” “Hours saved,” “Error rate,” and “Revenue impact.”
- Contrast a workflow with a positive return against one requiring expensive rework.
- On-screen text: **“Measure value, not just model price.”**

## Scene 13 — Will intelligence become cheap for everyone?
**Time:** 11:30–12:25

### Voiceover
So, will AI intelligence become cheap enough that everyone can use powerful systems for almost anything?

There are reasons to expect costs to keep falling. Chips improve, software gets more efficient, smaller models become more capable, and competition puts pressure on prices. Tasks that once required a premium model may eventually be handled by a much cheaper system.

But there are also forces pushing in the other direction. People may demand longer answers, more accurate reasoning, video and voice generation, real-time interaction, and agents that complete entire projects. Each new capability can create new computing needs. If usage expands quickly enough, total spending can rise even while the cost per task declines.

The future is therefore unlikely to be a simple story of AI getting either cheaper or more expensive. Basic tasks may become extremely affordable, while frontier-level reasoning, high-volume business automation, and complex agent workflows may remain costly.

The most important change may be that intelligence becomes easier to purchase in small units — much like cloud computing made computing power available on demand. But every unit still has an underlying cost.

### Visual direction
- Basic AI task becomes cheaper over time, while a complex agent workflow shows a larger resource meter.
- Owl compares “Simple task” and “Complex task.”
- On-screen text: **“The price of intelligence will depend on the job.”**

## Scene 14 — Final takeaway and call to action
**Time:** 12:25–13:15

### Voiceover
The next time an AI gives you a brilliant answer in seconds, remember what you cannot see: specialized chips, data centers, electricity, cooling, networking, research, software engineering, and a business model trying to turn all of it into a sustainable service.

AI can be incredibly useful. Its costs are also real, and they do not disappear just because the interface looks simple.

For consumers, this helps explain free tiers, usage limits, and differences between basic and premium models. For businesses, it is a reminder to measure the full cost of an AI workflow and compare it with the value it creates. And for investors, it is a reminder that rapid adoption does not automatically guarantee strong profits.

The central question is not whether AI is expensive or cheap. It is whether the intelligence delivered is worth more than the resources required to provide it.

## Production notes

- **Narration pace:** Aim for 145–155 words per minute. Allow short pauses for scene changes and on-screen statistics.
- **Visual rhythm:** Change the main visual every 5–8 seconds where practical, using slow camera moves, layered graphics, simple diagrams, and close-ups of the recurring owl character.
- **Mascot consistency:** Keep the owl's face, clothing, proportions, and overall design consistent. Change only its pose, expression, and props.
- **Music:** Use restrained technology/business background music; lower it under narration.
- **On-screen text:** Keep captions short. Use graphics for key ideas rather than displaying full paragraphs.
- **Fact-checking:** Treat industry estimates as estimates, state the year and scope, and avoid presenting a single token price as the universal cost of AI. Model prices and infrastructure estimates change quickly.
