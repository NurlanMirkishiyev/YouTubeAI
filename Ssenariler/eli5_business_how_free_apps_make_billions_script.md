# How Free Apps Make Billions Without Charging You
## ELI5 Business — 12-Minute YouTube Video Script

**Format:** Educational explainer with simple 2D animation and the ELI5 Business owl mascot  
**Target runtime:** Approximately 12 minutes, depending on narration pace and pauses  
**Narration language:** English

---

## 00:00–00:45 — Hook: If It’s Free, Who Pays?

**VISUAL:** A phone screen fills with Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, and mobile game icons. The owl mascot taps the screen. The icons transform into coins.

**NARRATION:**

Imagine an app you use every single day.

You download it for free. You watch videos, message your friends, share photos, and scroll for hours. You never pay a subscription. You never buy anything.

So here’s the big question: **if you’re not paying for the app, who is?**

Running an app isn’t free. Companies have to pay engineers, maintain servers, protect user accounts, and keep everything working.

Yet some of the world’s biggest technology companies offer free services to billions of people.

How does that make business sense?

The answer is more interesting than you might think. Once you understand how free apps make money, you may never look at your phone the same way again.

---

## 00:45–02:00 — Secret #1: Users Are an Opportunity

**VISUAL:** A small app starts with ten users. The number grows to one thousand, then one million, then millions of people connected through a network.

**NARRATION:**

Imagine you’re building a new social media app. What should your first goal be?

Should you immediately charge everyone five dollars a month? Or should you first give people a reason to join?

Many free apps choose the second option.

Why? Because people are more willing to try something when there’s no price tag.

Imagine asking someone to pay for a social network before they know whether their friends are there, whether the videos are interesting, or whether the app is useful.

That’s a tough sell.

But make it free, and joining becomes much easier. People invite friends. They share content. Other people join to see what everyone is talking about.

The app begins to grow.

A large audience gives the company opportunities to make money later. It might show ads, offer paid features, or sell subscriptions.

But there’s an important catch: **having lots of users doesn’t automatically mean making lots of money.**

The company still needs a business model that turns some part of that audience into revenue.

So how can millions of people using an app become valuable to someone else?

The first answer is advertising.

---

## 02:00–04:10 — Secret #2: Why Advertisers Pay

**VISUAL:** The owl watches a video. An ad for running shoes appears. On the other side of the screen, a shoe company allocates money to a digital advertising campaign.

**NARRATION:**

Let’s make this simple.

Imagine you sell running shoes. You’ve just launched a new collection, and you want more people to discover it.

You could put up a billboard. But what if you could show your ad to people who are already interested in running, fitness, or sports?

That’s where a digital platform can help.

The platform shows your ad to users. Some people notice it. Some click. A few might visit your store and buy a pair of shoes.

Depending on the advertising arrangement, you may pay for impressions, clicks, or other results.

The money flows from the advertiser to the platform. The user gets to keep using the app for free.

That’s one of the biggest secrets behind free apps: **the person using the product and the person paying for it don’t always have to be the same person.**

Consider Meta, the company behind Facebook and Instagram. In its 2025 reporting, Meta recorded roughly $201 billion in total revenue, with approximately $196 billion coming from advertising. Those figures cover Meta’s broader business, not Instagram alone.

That shows just how powerful advertising can be for a company with a huge audience.

But advertisers don’t simply want to reach as many people as possible. They want to reach the *right* people.

A company selling running shoes wants to reach people likely to care about running. A toy store may want to reach a completely different audience.

Platforms can use permitted signals about activity and interests to help make ads more relevant, subject to privacy rules and user settings.

TikTok’s advertising platform, for example, gives businesses tools to define audiences, set budgets, and measure campaign performance.

So the app isn’t just displaying random ads. It’s helping businesses find potential customers.

But this raises another question: why do so many apps try to keep us scrolling?

---

## 04:10–05:40 — Secret #3: Why Your Attention Is Valuable

**VISUAL:** The owl watches one video. Another starts automatically. The clock speeds up while more videos and occasional ads appear on the screen.

**NARRATION:**

Imagine opening an app to watch just one video.

The video ends. Another one appears. Then another. Soon, you see something funny, surprising, or useful, and you decide to watch for one more minute.

Suddenly, ten minutes have passed.

Why does this matter to the app?

Because when people spend more time on a platform, the company may have more opportunities to show content and ads. More opportunities can mean more revenue.

Think of a billboard on a quiet street compared with one on a road used by thousands of people every day. The busier location may be more attractive to advertisers.

Digital platforms work in a similar way, although more screen time does not automatically guarantee more money. Revenue also depends on ad prices, audience, ad placement, and how people respond.

That’s why many platforms try to recommend content that matches your interests.

If you watch cat videos, you may see more cat videos. If you’re interested in sports, you may see more sports content.

The goal is to keep the experience relevant enough that you want to return.

And that creates a valuable business asset.

**It’s not just the number of users that matters. It’s also a platform’s ability to earn and keep their attention.**

But advertising isn’t the only way a free app can make money. Sometimes, the user decides to pay directly.

---

## 05:40–07:10 — Secret #4: Freemium — Free for Everyone, Paid by Some

**VISUAL:** The screen splits in two. On the left, the owl uses a free photo-editing app. On the right, a premium version unlocks extra tools, advanced effects, and an ad-free experience.

**NARRATION:**

Imagine you use a free photo-editing app.

You can crop pictures, adjust colors, and apply basic filters without paying a cent.

Then one day, you want something more. Maybe you want to remove a background with artificial intelligence, unlock special effects, or use advanced editing tools.

The app offers you an upgrade: the basic features are free, but the extra features cost money.

This is called the **freemium model**—a combination of “free” and “premium.”

The idea is simple. Let everyone try the product, then offer additional value to people who want more.

One user might pay ten dollars a month for premium features. Another might be perfectly happy with the free version.

The company doesn’t need everyone to pay. It needs enough users to find the paid version valuable.

This model is common in software, music services, productivity tools, and games.

A free app might offer extra storage, exclusive content, advanced tools, or a subscription that removes ads.

Apple’s app ecosystem, for example, supports in-app purchases and subscriptions that let developers sell digital features and content within apps.

So why would someone pay for something they can already use for free?

Because the paid version may save time, offer more convenience, or help them achieve a better result.

And in some apps, small purchases can add up to a surprisingly large amount of revenue.

---

## 07:10–08:35 — Secret #5: How Small Purchases Add Up

**VISUAL:** A mobile game offers extra lives, character outfits, and virtual items. Small coins appear one by one, then combine into a larger pile.

**NARRATION:**

Now let’s look at mobile games.

You download a game for free and start playing. You complete levels, unlock rewards, and make progress.

Then the game offers you an extra life, a special outfit, a new character, or a way to speed things up.

These are examples of in-app purchases.

Some players never buy anything. Others make a few small purchases over the course of a month.

Let’s imagine a game has one million active users. Suppose just two percent of them spend five dollars in a particular month.

Two percent of one million is twenty thousand paying users.

Twenty thousand people spending five dollars each equals **one hundred thousand dollars in gross monthly revenue**.

**VISUAL:** 1,000,000 users → 20,000 paying users → $100,000 in monthly gross revenue.

This is a hypothetical example, not a claim about a real game. Actual results vary, and platform fees and other costs may reduce what the developer keeps.

But the principle is powerful: a company doesn’t need every user to spend money.

With a large enough audience, a relatively small group of paying users can generate meaningful revenue.

Some apps earn money through one-time purchases. Others rely on subscriptions that renew every month or year. Some combine subscriptions, advertising, and in-app purchases.

But all these models work better when people keep using the app.

So how do companies encourage users to come back?

---

## 08:35–09:50 — Secret #6: Why Apps Want You to Return

**VISUAL:** A notification appears on the phone. The owl opens the app. A calendar flips through several days as the user returns repeatedly.

**NARRATION:**

Creating an app is one challenge. Getting people to use it regularly is another.

Imagine a company spends money hiring developers, building features, and attracting thousands of users.

But everyone opens the app once and then forgets about it.

The company may have to spend more money to attract new users, without getting much value from the people it already reached.

Now imagine people return every day because the app is useful, entertaining, or helps them stay connected.

The company has more opportunities to serve those users and earn revenue over time.

That’s why apps may use new content, useful features, personalized recommendations, and notifications to encourage people to return.

Of course, not every notification is designed simply to make money. Some genuinely help users keep up with things they care about.

The business logic is straightforward: people find value in the app, they keep using it, and the company has more chances to earn revenue through ads, subscriptions, or other services.

A successful business can create a cycle. A useful product attracts users. Those users create opportunities to earn revenue. Some of that revenue can then be invested in improving the product.

But there’s a potential downside.

If a company focuses only on increasing screen time, its business goals can sometimes conflict with a user’s well-being.

So the question isn’t only how much money an app makes. It’s also how much genuine value it provides.

And that brings us to one final misconception.

---

## 09:50–11:05 — Secret #7: Free Doesn’t Mean Cost-Free

**VISUAL:** Behind a phone, a server room, developers, security systems, and electricity meters appear. The owl compares a revenue stack with an expense stack.

**NARRATION:**

We’ve seen how free apps can make money. But there’s something we shouldn’t forget: revenue is not the same as profit.

Behind an app, there may be software engineers, designers, servers, cybersecurity teams, customer support, and marketing expenses.

All of that costs money.

Imagine an app earns one hundred thousand dollars in a month. It spends ninety thousand dollars on servers, staff, marketing, and other operating costs.

Before taxes and any other unaccounted-for expenses, that leaves ten thousand dollars.

In other words, generating a lot of revenue doesn’t automatically mean a business is highly profitable. The company has to manage its costs and maintain a sustainable business model.

And not every free app is designed to make money directly. Some exist to provide public services, educate people, promote a brand, or support another business. Others may not generate revenue at all.

So it would be wrong to assume that every free app uses the same business model.

Some combine several approaches: advertising, premium subscriptions, and paid features. Others rely on just one main source of income.

The most important thing is that the company has a reason to offer the service for free—and a plan for how the service can support itself.

Now, let’s put the whole story together.

---

## 11:05–12:00 — Final: What Are You Really Paying For?

**VISUAL:** The owl puts down the phone. Four icons appear: a user, an advertiser, a premium subscription, and an in-app purchase. The ELI5 Business logo appears at the end.

**NARRATION:**

The next time you open a free app, remember that giving you free access may be only one part of a much bigger business model.

The company might sell advertising to businesses. It might charge for extra features. It might offer a subscription. Or it might be focused on growing its user base for another reason.

Every app is different.

But here’s the big lesson:

**A free product isn’t always the end of the business. Sometimes, it’s the entrance to a much bigger business model.**

A company may first earn people’s attention and trust. Then it tries to create value for users, advertisers, or both—and turn that value into revenue.

Now I have a question for you.

Which business model do you think is stronger: charging everyone a small amount, or giving millions of people a free service and earning money in other ways?

Share your answer in the comments.

Because understanding business isn’t just about knowing where the money goes. It’s about understanding **why it goes there**.
